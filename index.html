Architectural overview of the LLM-Guided Tool-Aware TAMP framework. The hierarchy integrates symbolic reasoning (LLM Module) with physical execution (TAMP Module), supported by a multi-modal perception interface (Perception Module) and a robotic skill library (Skill Library Module).

Abstract

Autonomous chemistry laboratories can accelerate material discovery and improve experimental reproducibility. However, current systems are limited by rigid tool configurations and unreliable motion planning under orientation and collision constraints, and language-model-based planners stop at protocol generation without reasoning about execution context. This paper proposes a hierarchical, tool-aware task and motion planning framework for chemistry automation that couples large-language-model reasoning with batched differentiable optimization. The system converts natural-language instructions into validated chemical description language protocols, infers end-effectors and obstacle rearrangement strategies from procedural context, estimates the scene state through multi-view fiducial markers, and optimizes collision-free, constraint-satisfying trajectories in parallel, while a sensor-driven skill library handles tool changing and adaptive pouring. On a six-degree-of-freedom manipulator, the planner solves every configuration of three constrained tasks within the time budget, with each optimization round taking a narrowly bounded time per task, and on the task whose orientation constraint is most restrictive it reaches a 100.0% planning success rate against 73.3% for a sequential parameter-binding baseline. In randomized trials and ablation studies in an NVIDIA Isaac Sim testbed, the full framework executes complete chemical sequences involving tool switching, obstacle rearrangement, and multi-step reagent manipulation. On the physical robot, adaptive pouring reached a mean absolute error of 0.19–0.34 g for water and a syrup of about 3,000 cP and 0.36–0.73 g for powdered sugar over target masses of 10–50 g, and the Transfer procedure planned from the perceived positions was completed in all nine runs.

Methodology

The framework addresses three limitations of chemistry laboratory automation with four modules. The LLM Module converts natural-language instructions into validated XDL protocols and determines the execution context of each procedure, namely end-effector selection and obstacle rearrangement. The TAMP Module adopts cuTAMP as its planning backbone for constrained motion planning with bounded latency. The Skill Library Module handles through sensor feedback the execution-level actions that geometric planning alone cannot, such as adaptive pouring and temperature regulation. The Perception Module underpins all three by constructing the World State.

LLM Module

Protocol Generation, Validation & Context-Aware Action Reasoning

The LLM Module proceeds through three stages. The XDL Generator, a fine-tuned Llama 3.2 1B model, converts a natural-language command into a complete XDL protocol in a single pass. The XDL Validator then applies three progressively stricter checks, namely syntactic, object-existence, and physical-feasibility validation, which together verify 25 enumerated conditions before any robot motion is executed. Before each procedure, the Action Reasoner, a second fine-tuned Llama 3.2 1B model, reads the current and next procedures and the World State and infers four discrete outputs: the main end-effector, whether obstacle rearrangement is necessary, the auxiliary tool for rearrangement, and the target grid among 12 workspace grids. The LLM is confined to these discrete symbolic outputs, and the TAMP Module resolves the exact placement in continuous space, which keeps hallucinated spatial quantities out of the planning pipeline.

Perception Module

Multi-View World-State Grounding

The Perception Module estimates the 6-DoF poses of laboratory assets from AprilTag markers affixed to each vessel. Two calibrated RGB cameras at separated positions estimate the poses continuously, and their estimates are fused by inverse-distance weighting with SLERP for orientation. When only one camera sees a marker, its estimate is used directly. When neither fixed camera can read a marker or its measurement cannot be trusted, the arm carries a wrist camera through predefined viewpoints to search for it, and if the wrist camera cannot read it either, execution stops and the object is reported to the user. The poses are assembled into the World State at every procedure boundary, with objects approximated by primitives covered with spheres for collision checking. The module also filters the scale and temperature streams that the closed-loop skills consume.

TAMP Module

Batched Joint Differentiable Optimization with Chemistry-Specific Operators

The TAMP Module adopts cuTAMP as its planning solver, jointly optimizing all constraints across a large batch of candidate particles by gradient-based updates. Because a fixed number of optimization steps runs over a fixed particle budget, one optimization round takes a narrowly bounded time, and a round that finds no solution is restarted with fresh particles, so planning latency can be estimated in units of rounds. Four chemistry-specific operators are introduced: SidePick for full-circumference grasping of cylindrical vessels, MoveHolding for upright-constrained transport within a tilt bound of 5°, Pour for the geometric tilting trajectory that hands over to the pouring skill, and Stir for device-level stirrer control delegated to the execution layer.

Skill Library Module

Sensor-Feedback-Based Motion Primitives

The Skill Library Module instantiates nominal TAMP trajectories into sensor-feedback-driven motion primitives. Adaptive Pouring estimates the flow rate as the slope of a line fitted to the recent scale readings. It detects the angle at which the reagent starts to flow on every pour, adjusts the tilt to follow a target flow rate proportional to the amount remaining, and returns the vessel below that angle once the measured weight plus the predicted amount still in flight reaches the target. Automatic Tool Changing executes electromagnet control and mechanical latching at the tool rack. Feedback-Based Device Control monitors temperature and RPM streams and suspends execution until the target physical conditions are met.

XDL Generator. The 5,000 training samples span synonym variations for the six core XDL operators (Add, Stir, HeatChill, Transfer, CleanVessel, Move), numerical parameter ranges (0.5–20 mL in steps of 0.5 mL, 0–20 °C, 1–30 s), three instance identifiers for each of four vessel types (12 identifiers), and command complexity of 30% single-step, 40% two-step, and 30% three-step procedures. Training, validation, and evaluation commands are separated at five levels. Of 104 sentence templates, 62 are used in training, 12 in a development set, and 30 in evaluation, divided into 18 with unseen wording and 12 with unseen sentence forms. One of the four names of every vessel type appears only in evaluation. The numerical values of each range are split into disjoint pools, and three ordered operator pairs (CleanVessel → Add, HeatChill → Transfer, Stir → Move) are withheld from training. No command occurs in both the training and the evaluation data.

Action Reasoner. The 3,000 training samples place up to four objects on 12 workspace grids and are balanced across rearrangement-required and rearrangement-unnecessary cases, and across context-aware and irrelevant obstacles. Training and evaluation scenarios are separated along three axes. For objects, the fourth instance name of each type (for example, beaker_C) is reserved for evaluation, and the test tube and tray types are not used in training. For spatial relations, 8 ordered grid pairs of target and obstacle (G3–G8, G4–G9, G5–G10, G6–G11, and their reverses) are withheld. For task combinations, 6 pairs of current and next operators (Add → Stir, Stir → Transfer, HeatChill → Move, Transfer → HeatChill, CleanVessel → Add, Move → CleanVessel) are withheld, while every operator still appears in training in both positions.

Over 2,300 test commands in seven evaluation sets, only 34 generated protocols differ from their reference. Argument names and units agree in every set, and exact match stays at 97.7–100.0% on the sets that differ from the training distribution at one level and 93.3% on the set that differs at all five. The XDL Validator rejects every one of the 625 protocols that violate one of its 25 conditions, rejects none of the 300 that must pass or the 8,100 reference protocols, and blocks 30 of the 34 errors that the generator actually produced.

LLM-Based Tool Selection & Context-Aware Rearrangement

The Action Reasoner assigns the correct end-effector in all 1,600 unseen scenarios, including object types absent from the fine-tuning data, and decides rearrangement necessity correctly under every condition. Over the 400 situations in which the next procedure requires a particular station, context-aware rearrangement reduces the mean number of manipulations by 37.5% against random rearrangement. On Transfer → Stir it reaches 96.67% sequence completion against 50.0% for random rearrangement and 0% without rearrangement.

Validation on the Physical FR5

The execution layer and the Perception Module are validated on the physical FR5 with the 2-finger gripper, the two fixed cameras and the wrist camera, and an electronic scale (0.01 g, 5 Hz) under the receiving beaker. Three reagents represent a low-viscosity liquid, a high-viscosity liquid, and a granular reagent: water, an oligosaccharide syrup of about 3,000 cP at 26 °C, and powdered sugar.

Pouring Accuracy

The source beaker held in the gripper is tilted from a fixed start pose, and each reagent is poured 10 times at 10, 30, and 50 g. Water and the syrup reached within ±1.0 g of the target in all 60 pours, with a mean absolute error of 0.19–0.34 g. The syrup is about 3,000 times more viscous than water yet reached the same accuracy, because the pouring skill estimates the flow rate directly from the scale readings. A syrup pour took 52–86 s against 40–66 s for water. Powdered sugar reached within ±1.0 g in 23 of 30 pours and was biased toward overshoot, because powder slides down in clumps rather than flowing continuously.

Physical Execution of the Transfer Procedure

In each run the planning module plans the trajectory from the positions of the two beakers measured by the Perception Module, and the robot picks up the source beaker, carries it upright, pours the target mass with the pouring skill, and returns the beaker to its place. One run was made for each of the nine combinations of the three reagents and 10, 30, and 50 g, with the source beaker moved between runs by up to 65 mm, and all nine runs completed the procedure. The trajectories excluding the pour were executed as planned without any position correction, and in every run the pouring skill took over the transfer from the planned start pose.
